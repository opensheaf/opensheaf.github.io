# Living knowledge in a time of abundance

Science and mathematics are entering a period of abundance. AI systems, automated provers, simulations and large-scale experiments now produce results faster than any community can read them: lemmas, conjectures, computations, datasets, partial proofs, negative results. Much of this output is raw. Some of it is right, some is wrong, most of it has not been checked; it is scattered, redundant, written in many idioms, and rarely connected to what is already known. A fact that cannot be found, compared or trusted adds little to understanding, however true it may be.

Turning such material into knowledge has always been intellectual work: distillation, unification and normalisation. A survey reorganises a field; a textbook chooses definitions; a referee checks a claim and places it; a formal library restates a result so that others can build on it. Abundance makes this work more necessary and harder, not obsolete.

It also matters to machines. Systems that learn from the scientific record and reason over it are only as good as what they read. They need a clean signal, aligned across sources: texts whose versions are fixed, whose authors and origins are known, and whose relations to other texts (correction, extension, endorsement, disagreement) are recorded rather than guessed. Fed instead on raw output, much of it their own, they will return it amplified. Knowledge that serves both people and machines is therefore not a static archive. It is living: revised, reorganised and argued over, while keeping a faithful memory of what was said, by whom and when.

No single institution can or should do this work. Distilling involves choices: which definitions, which presentation, which results matter and how they fit together. Different schools, communities and individuals will reasonably choose differently. Academic freedom means being able to take the same material and explore it in several ways: to reorganise a field from another point of view, to continue someone else's work in another direction, to select what one thinks deserves attention. A central infrastructure, however well meant, tends to fix one reading and one set of gatekeepers. A federated, decentralised one lets many readings coexist.

Freedom without exchange, however, is fragmentation. Science is a shared and accountable activity: claims must be attributable, versions comparable, corrections visible, disagreements traceable. Divergent lines of work on the same subject must be able to cite one another precisely, and every reader, human or machine, must be able to tell which version of a text a statement concerns and who stands behind it. The infrastructure must allow divergence and keep dialogue possible at the same time.

OpenSheaf (<https://opensheaf.org/>) is an open format that tries to provide such an infrastructure. Authors and communities keep their works in archives of plain files that any web server can host. Each version is fixed and signed by its owners, and a work keeps one name across its versions and its hosts. Others can continue a work in their own name and on their own responsibility, as a branch of the same work, without interfering with the original. Journals, overlays and personal reading lists become collections that point to exact versions in other people's archives instead of holding copies, and archives can copy one another to preserve what they rely on or wish to support. The aim is not one more place to publish, but a common, verifiable ground on which many independent efforts of distillation can build, disagree and stay in conversation: the ground that living knowledge needs.
