# Hexagon and OpenSheaf

*Working paper, draft of 21 November 2026. Non-normative: ideas and positions,
not decisions. Hexagon is a repository for research in the mathematical
sciences and theoretical computer science, opened in September 2026 by a
nonprofit foundation, which accepts works written in part or entirely by
language models and records how they were made. This paper describes what it
offers, sets it against OpenSheaf function by function (in the division of
[positioning.md](positioning.md)) and point by point, and evaluates the
position of each. Of the services compared so far it is the closest to
OpenSheaf in purpose: a record, with versions, open licences and digests of
files. It differs in where trust lies. The paper ends with proposals,
experiments and open questions, and continues the comparison with
[alphaXiv](alphaxiv.md).*

## 1. The question

The [positioning paper](positioning.md) asks for a record that others can
check and build on, kept apart from the services that announce, filter and
evaluate. The [alphaXiv paper](alphaxiv.md) looked at a service that grew
from reading into recording, and found the record its weakest part. Hexagon
starts from the other end: it is a record first, made by mathematicians for a
problem that arXiv chose not to take on, the growing number of results proved
with, or by, language models. Two questions follow. How close is a careful,
community-run repository to what OpenSheaf asks of a record, and what is
left? And does the arrival of AI-generated mathematics change what a format
for the record should carry?

## 2. What Hexagon is

**Who runs it.** The Hexagon Mathematics Foundation, a nonprofit
incorporated in Delaware, which intends to apply for tax-exempt status. Its
executive director is Benjamin Antieau; its board Mohammed Abouzaid
(president), Francois Charles, Bryna Kra, David Savitt and Lauren Williams;
its advisory board Kevin Buzzard, Akhil Mathew, Johannes Schmitt, Steinn
Sigurdsson, Nikhil Srivastava, Ravi Vakil and Rachel Ward. "So far, it is
entirely funded by the people involved" (Antieau). It was built in August
and September 2026, has accepted submissions publicly since 28 September,
and is in public beta.

**Why.** In Antieau's account, arXiv was not going to be the home of results
found with language models, and the founders wanted "a lower bar of
mathematical significance and novelty" than arXiv's. Chafai calls it "a
specialized, newer complement, not a replacement". A work "may be posted to
both".

**Scope.** "Research-level works in the mathematical sciences and theoretical
computer science, including both original and expository texts", in arXiv's
subject classes (all of `math`, part of `cs`, `math-ph`, `quant-ph`,
`stat.TH`), with MSC codes. Small, incremental results are welcome.

**What is accepted.** Works "entirely generated by LLMs and even where the
submitters claim no human understanding" (Antieau). The contributors "must
reasonably believe that the work is correct and of interest to the
mathematical community", and confirm it, with their citations, at
submission. Acceptance "is not a judgment about its correctness, quality, or
novelty". Each deposit is screened automatically (it compiles, it is a
scholarly contribution, it is not abusive), and during the beta by a human
moderator, within 48 hours on weekdays; "incorrect proofs of famous
conjectures will often be rejected without comment".

**How a work is made, recorded.** Each version records:

- `authorshipMode`: `human`, `mixed` or `no-human-author-claimed`, "not a
  claim that an AI system is an author";
- `humanUnderstanding`: `all`, `some` or `none`, with a `provenance`
  statement, in free text, required when it is `some`;
- `tools`: the AI systems used, by provider, name and version;
- optionally, supplementary files such as prompts and transcripts, and
  links to formalisations in Mathlib, Palomar, Prove2.me or Tau Ceti.
  Formal code itself is not accepted as part of a submission.

"Artificial-intelligence systems cannot be contributors": contributors are
people and organisations, identified by ORCID iDs. AI laboratories can have
verified accounts.

**Who submits.** Anyone signed in with an ORCID iD, ordinarily a contributor,
others with Hexagon's authorisation and the contributors' permission. A new
account may deposit one work a day, more as its works are accepted.

**Identifiers and versions.** `hexagon:YYMM.NNNNN`, with a version
(`hexagon:2611.00802v1`), at `https://hexagonmath.org/<id>`. "Every version
of a work has its own stable, permanent identifier", and once public a
version stays "part of the scholarly record, even if the submission is later
revised or withdrawn". Some fields are changed after publication without a
new version: the arXiv identifier (which "Hexagon does not verify"),
formalisations and tags. DOIs are planned, not yet assigned.

**Withdrawal and removal.** A withdrawn work "remains available but will be
marked with a notice of the withdrawal", with a reason. Hexagon may remove a
work from public access for a submission error, missing consent of
contributors, or privacy and rights; "Removal from Hexagon does not
guarantee deletion of copies held elsewhere."

**Rights.** Contributors keep their copyright and choose a licence among CC
BY 4.0, CC BY-SA 4.0 and CC0 1.0. They grant Hexagon "a worldwide,
non-exclusive, perpetual, irrevocable, royalty-free license to reproduce,
host, preserve, back up, make available, and distribute", transferable if
another organisation takes over the repository, and which allows sending
copies to other archives and preservation services.

**Access.** Pages, PDF and sources (`/src/<id>`, a ZIP, and the files one by
one); BibTeX; RSS feeds by subject; an arXiv-compatible Atom query interface
(`/api/query`); a JSON API (`/api/v1/works`, filtered by subject, MSC,
tool, `humanUnderstanding` and more, paged by cursor); a read-only MCP
server; and an API for submissions with tokens. The record of a version lists
its files, each with its size and **SHA-256 digest**. There is no OAI-PMH
endpoint and no bulk download in the API description; harvesting is by
paging the query interface or the JSON API.

**Not found.** Comments or discussion (feedback goes to the foundation by
mail, a report form or a GitHub repository); signatures; a digest of a
version as a whole; a named preservation partner; whether its software is
open source.

## 3. Function by function

In the division of [positioning.md](positioning.md), section 1:

| Function | Hexagon | OpenSheaf |
|---|---|---|
| Record | yes: dated versions with permanent identifiers, provenance of each version, SHA-256 of each file | the form of the record: object versions fixed by SHA-512 inventories, signed by their owners, named by `sheaf:` URIs |
| Preserve | a decision to preserve each accepted work; a transferable licence; no named partner | none by itself; checkable copies (section 11), so that others can preserve |
| Announce | yes: listings by month, RSS feeds by subject, an arXiv-style API | the raw material: an archive's feed (section 3.1, appendix A.3); aggregation is a service |
| Filter | screening only, on purpose; filters on provenance (`humanUnderstanding`, tools, formalisations) | the form of a filter's statements: selections pinning the revisions vetted (section 8.4) |
| Distribute | yes, to people, programs and agents (API, MCP) | any web server; landing pages for search engines (appendix A.3); the Explorer |
| Assign merit | none, on purpose | none counted; the evidence made checkable |

Hexagon is a bundle of record, announcement and distribution, as arXiv is,
and declines to filter or judge, as arXiv mostly does. What is new is the
provenance it records, which is the material that filters of AI-generated
work will need.

## 4. Point by point

**Fixity.** Hexagon publishes a SHA-256 digest of every file of a version,
which is more than arXiv or alphaXiv shows. But the digests are served by
the same host as the files, and nothing fixes the set: there is no digest of
the version as a whole, so a citation cannot carry a pin, and a reader who
trusts a copy of a file has only Hexagon's word that it belongs to that
version. In OpenSheaf the inventory lists every file with its SHA-512, and
its own digest is the pin (sections 4 and 7.6): one value fixes the version,
can be written in a citation (`?sha512=`, section 12.1), and is checked
against any copy from anywhere. A Hexagon version could be made checkable
in this sense with little: a manifest of its files, and a digest of the
manifest published with the identifier.

**Who stands behind a version.** On Hexagon, an account signed in with an
ORCID iD; the version is authentic because Hexagon says who deposited it.
In OpenSheaf, an owner's keys sign each version (section 7), and the
signature is checked without asking the host; ORCID enters only as a keeper
who answers for an owner (section 6.6). For a repository that wants to be
trusted while it lasts, the first is enough; for a record that should be
believed after its host has gone, or in copies kept elsewhere, it is not.

**Names.** `hexagon:2611.00802v1` is a name in one repository, as
`arXiv:2611.00802v1` is, resolved by it; DOIs will add a resolver run by
DataCite. A `sheaf:` URI is resolved from any copy that passes the checks
(section 12.3). The forms are close (a work, a version number), and a
Hexagon identifier fits as an external label of an OpenSheaf version
(`external`, section 4.3).

**Versions.** Close in intent: every version kept and citable, withdrawal
leaving the record in place. Hexagon changes some fields of a published
version in place (the arXiv identifier, formalisations, tags); in
OpenSheaf any change is a new object version, a description-only one when
the document does not change (section 4.2), so that what a version said
can be read back. Links to formalisations are exactly the kind of field
that grows after publication; what a description-only version may change
is open in OpenSheaf too
([#24](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/24)).

**Withdrawal and removal.** Hexagon's withdrawal is OpenSheaf's (section
13.1): the work stays, marked, with a reason. Its removal is a takedown
(section 13.2); the pages do not say what stays at the address of a removed
work, and nothing like a tombstone with a pin is described. Hexagon says plainly that copies held elsewhere are not its
business, which is also where OpenSheaf is undecided
([#33](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/33),
[#66](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/66)).

**Rights.** Hexagon's three licences, CC BY, CC BY-SA and CC0, all allow
redistribution: every work on Hexagon meets the condition on licences that
OpenSheaf sets for mirrors (section 11.2) and the one proposed in
[#34](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/34). Its own
licence, transferable to a successor, and its explicit permission to send
copies to other archives, are what a custodian needs. Of the services
compared so far, it is the one whose whole holdings could be kept by others
without asking anyone.

**Preservation.** A decision to preserve, a licence that allows it, and no
named partner yet. OpenSheaf promises no more, and relies on custodians whose
promises are open
([#54](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/54)). Both
lack the institution that lasts.

**Provenance of AI-generated work.** Hexagon's main contribution, and one
OpenSheaf has nothing for. `authorshipMode`, `humanUnderstanding`,
`provenance` and `tools` are statements of the contributors about how a
version was made, recorded per version and searchable. OpenSheaf's
`toolchain` (section 4.3) lists the tools that build the renditions, not
those that wrote the mathematics, and DataCite has no fields for it. Such a
statement belongs to a version, should be fixed with it, and, since it is a
claim, should be signed by those who make it: three things OpenSheaf does
and Hexagon does not.

**Formalisation.** Hexagon links formal proofs (Mathlib, Palomar,
Prove2.me, Tau Ceti) but does not hold them. A link to a formalisation says
that a proof exists somewhere, not which version of the paper it checks,
nor which version of the proof. In OpenSheaf, a formalisation can be a
document of its own, versioned, with a relation to the version it
formalises, and a statement that it checks can be a member of a selection
pinning both. Whether a formal proof is a document, a dataset or software,
and how relations between them are written, is open
([#21](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/21)).

**Discussion.** Neither has any. Hexagon takes feedback privately;
OpenSheaf's question is open
([#21](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/21)).

**Machine readers.** Hexagon has a JSON API with an OpenAPI description, an
arXiv-compatible query interface, an MCP server and an API for submissions,
all documented, public for reading. OpenSheaf archives are files that a
program can read, with indexes (section 12.5), but no service answers
questions over them.

**Openness.** A nonprofit, run by working mathematicians, with open
licences and a public API, but one repository: one host, one database, one
set of identifiers. OpenSheaf is a format with open tools, and its archives
are static files that anyone can copy, check and serve.

## 5. Evaluating the positions

### What Hexagon does well

- **It answers a real need quickly.** Results found with language models
  are arriving now, and had no home; Hexagon gave them one in two months,
  with rules.
- **It makes provenance a field.** Who or what wrote the text, how much of
  it a human understood, which systems were used: recorded per version,
  queryable, the basis on which others can filter. None of the other
  services compared here records it per version.
- **It is honest about what acceptance means.** Screening is not review,
  and says so; the record of a version is separated from any judgement of
  it. This is the separation of record and filter that
  [positioning.md](positioning.md) argues for, done inside one repository.
- **Its rights allow copying.** Open licences only, a perpetual licence
  that passes to a successor, copies to other archives allowed.
- **Its community.** Run by a foundation whose board and advisers are known
  mathematicians, with formalisation people among them.
- **Its interfaces.** An arXiv-compatible API means existing tools work;
  per-file digests are already published.

### Where its position is weak

- **One host.** Everything rests on one repository in beta, funded by its
  founders. Its licence allows copies, but nothing in its record lets a
  copy be checked as a whole version without it.
- **Trust by account.** Provenance statements are claims; they are
  recorded under an account, not signed by those who make them, and can be
  checked only by asking Hexagon who deposited what.
- **Mutable fields.** Some metadata of a published version changes in
  place, with no record of what it said before.
- **Filtering is left to others, who have nothing to work with.** Hexagon
  rightly declines to judge, but provides no form in which someone else's
  judgement (a check by a referee, a formalisation completed, an error found)
  could be stated about a version and found from it.
- **Volume.** AI-generated mathematics can grow faster than any reading;
  the one-a-day limit and screening slow it, but the problem of what
  deserves attention is only moved to whoever filters.

### Where OpenSheaf's position is weak

- **No community, no content.** Hexagon had works within weeks; OpenSheaf
  has one real archive.
- **Nothing for provenance.** It has no place for what Hexagon records, the
  thing that matters most about AI-generated work.
- **Cost to contributors.** Keys and hosting, against an ORCID sign-in.
- **No service.** No API to query across archives, no feeds aggregated, no
  resolver to call.

### The relation

Hexagon is the kind of archive OpenSheaf was designed to hold, kept by the
kind of party it was designed for: a community institution, with open
licences, versions that stay, withdrawal that keeps the record. It would
lose nothing by being an OpenSheaf archive, and gain what it lacks: a pin for
each version, signatures for what its contributors state, copies that
others can keep and check, and a form in which filters (referees,
formalisers, overlay journals) can state what they vetted. OpenSheaf, for
its part, should learn from Hexagon what a record of AI-generated work needs
to say.

The two can meet without Hexagon changing anything, since its licences allow
copies:

- **Hexagon's works imported into OpenSheaf archives.** A contributor can
  keep a version deposited on Hexagon in an archive of their own, as a
  document version of their line, with Hexagon's record as an external
  record of role `source` (section 4.3) and the Hexagon identifier as its
  label, and check the files against Hexagon's SHA-256 digests on import.
- **Hexagon as an archive.** The foundation could publish its holdings as
  an OpenSheaf archive: one owner for the repository, or the contributors'
  own owners, with its listings as catalogues. The feasibility of the
  first, whose signatures would say only that Hexagon holds a version, not
  who wrote it, is a question in itself.
- **Selections over Hexagon.** A selection of works checked by a referee, or
  whose formalisation is complete, pins exactly the versions concerned. Its
  members are lines, so the works are first imported (above), and a
  selection that keeps its own copies keeps accepted copies of them
  (section 9.6); the licences allow both.

## 6. Proposals

These are ideas for discussion, not decisions; each would go through an
issue before anything changes.

- **Provenance of a version.** Open an issue on recording how a version was
  made: whether OpenSheaf should define fields like Hexagon's
  (`authorshipMode`, `humanUnderstanding`, `tools`, a provenance text,
  supplementary transcripts), as an extension or a profile, in `object.json`
  or in `datacite.json`; how they relate to `toolchain`; and whether such a
  statement is signed by the owner only, or also by the contributors who
  make it. Hexagon's values are a tested starting point, and taking them as
  they are would let records move between the two.
- **A Hexagon profile.** A profile beside Zenodo's (appendix A.1): import a
  Hexagon version as a document version (files checked against its SHA-256
  digests, metadata mapped to DataCite, provenance kept), and record the
  Hexagon identifier in `external`. Hexagon's API for submissions with tokens
  would also allow deposit in the other direction.
- **Formalisations as documents.** Use cases for a paper and its formal
  proof as two lines with relations, and a selection stating that a given
  version of the proof checks a given version of the paper, for
  [#21](https://github.com/opensheaf/opensheaf.github.io/issues/21).
- **A suggestion to Hexagon.** Publish, for each version, a manifest of its
  files with their digests, and a digest of the manifest beside the
  identifier, so that a version can be cited by a pin and checked anywhere.
  An OCFL inventory is such a manifest, and would make every Hexagon version
  readable as an OpenSheaf object version without anything else.

## 7. Experiments

- Import one Hexagon work (CC BY or CC0) into a test archive with `osheaf`:
  fetch the record and the files through `/api/v1`, check the SHA-256
  digests, map the metadata to `datacite.json`, keep the provenance, and see
  what is lost.
- Harvest a month of Hexagon (`/api/query` or `/api/v1/works`) and count
  works by `humanUnderstanding`, `authorshipMode`, tool and licence: a
  picture of what the record of AI-generated mathematics looks like.
- Build a catalogue of the Hexagon works of one subject, as an OpenSheaf
  collection, and see what the Explorer shows.
- Check what happens on Hexagon to a work also posted to arXiv: whether
  `arxivId` is filled, and whether the two records name the same files.

## 8. Open questions

- Is provenance a property of a version (fixed with it, as Hexagon records
  it) or a statement about it that may change (as understanding grows, as a
  proof is formalised)? Probably both, which would put the first in the
  version and the second in notices or collections.
- Should a format distinguish AI-generated works at all, or is that a
  matter for the archives and filters that care?
- Can an archive sign for works it hosts but did not write, and what would
  such a signature mean to a reader?
- Who will filter AI-generated mathematics, and in what form will they
  publish what they checked?

## 9. Conclusion

Hexagon is the most OpenSheaf-like of the services compared so far: a
community record, with versions that stay, open licences, a transferable
licence for preservation, digests of files and a public API. What it lacks
is what OpenSheaf is for: a pin for each version, signatures that make
claims checkable without the host, and copies and filters that work without
asking it. What OpenSheaf lacks is what Hexagon is for: a community with
content, and a record of how a work was made. The most useful step for
OpenSheaf is to take provenance seriously, starting from Hexagon's fields;
the most useful step for Hexagon, should it want one, is a digest of each
version, which would make its records checkable anywhere and readable as
OpenSheaf objects.
